Camera angle assessment · pre-registered

Can this angle carry a football analytics pipeline?

Three clips run end to end through the footy pipeline and scored against criteria fixed before the first frame was processed. The headline: the vision half works better than expected. The measurement half does not work at all, and no amount of configuration will fix it.

1Verdict

Decision rules were written before the run, so this verdict is mechanical rather than editorial.

Tactical / shape

Usable — 2 of 3 clips

clip0 and clip1 clear every gate. clip2 misses on team coverage (56.1 % vs 60 % required).

Physical metrics

Blocked

No distance, speed or sprint counts. Not a tuning problem — the camera moves, so there is no metric frame of reference.

Event data

Blocked

Depends on metres for its coordinates. Falls with §5.1. Hand-tagging remains the honest route.

Detector quality

Better than spec

Finds 15.0 people per frame against a hand count of 15. The feared crowd contamination did not happen.

The one thing to take away

This angle cannot produce metres, and therefore cannot produce any physical or event metric. A single fixed homography — the obvious workaround — exceeds its own 2-metre error budget after 0.8–1.2 seconds of footage, and sits around 10–11 m of positional error thereafter. Anything reporting a player's distance covered from this angle would be fabricated.

2What was tested

2.1Material

Three watermarked iStock/Getty stock clips of rendered football, treated — as instructed — as one candidate real camera angle. All 768×432 at 25 fps. Camera motion was measured by phase correlation before anything else, because it determines what is possible.

Pan is peak excursion at half resolution, so the true figures are roughly double. All three cameras pan continuously; none is locked off. That single measurement drives everything in §5.

2.2Method

Criteria were frozen in a pre-registration document before the pipeline ran, with numeric thresholds and two falsifiable predictions. Thresholds were anchored to things that already existed — the repo's own declared expectation of players_visible: 15 for this camera class, and its own 2-metre reprojection warning bound — rather than to anything observed in the output.

Detection recall needed an independent yardstick, so on-pitch people were counted by hand on one frame per clip from 2.4× upscaled stills, before the detector was run. Spectators, stewards and photographers were excluded; that boundary is exactly what the crowd-contamination criterion tests.

4What works

4.1Detection — the surprise

The pre-registered worry was crowd bleed. These stadiums are packed; a COCO person detector has no concept of "on the pitch", and the stands hold thousands of human figures. If that leaked, person counts would run to the hundreds and every downstream number would be worthless.

It did not happen. At 768×432 a spectator is a handful of pixels and falls below the 0.35 confidence gate, while a player at 30–40 px clears it. The resolution that makes shirt numbers unreadable is the same property that filters the crowd for free.

Caveat an analyst should hold on to

Aggregate agreement hides a composition error. On clip0 frame 0 the detector returns exactly 15 boxes against a hand count of 15 — but one is an off-pitch steward labelled away, and the yellow referee is left unassigned. Officials are the weak point, and a mislabelled official silently inflates a team's count.

4.2Tracking — passes on quality, fails on tidiness

Track continuity passes comfortably: median lifetimes of 102, 46 and 50 frames against a required 25. The identity-churn ceiling failed on two clips — clip1 produced 179 track ids for ~18.5 people on screen.

That reads worse than it is. The fragmentation lives in a tail of short-lived spurious tracks: 93–97 % of all detection rows belong to tracks that survive two seconds or more. For possession-level shape work the usable spine is intact; for anything needing a player followed continuously through a whole sequence, it is not.

4.3Team assignment — correct on all three

The kit-colour clustering resolved home and away correctly on every clip, verified numerically rather than by eye. Coverage was 77 %, 63 % and 56 % of tracks; clip2's 56 % is the one genuine miss.

A finding worth carrying to real footage: clip2 found no referee at all. Its configured referee colour is #303030, a near-neutral dark grey. The method keys on Lab chroma, and a neutral kit has almost none — it is invisible to a chroma-only matcher by construction. Yellow and olive officials in the other two clips were found without trouble. When choosing officials' kit for a real deployment, avoid neutrals.

4.4Ball — adequate on two clips, marginal on the third

Ball presence reached 41.5 % and 46.6 % of frames on clip0 and clip1, against a deliberately low 20 % bar. clip2 landed at 19.2 % — a fail by eight thousandths, and reported as a fail because the threshold was fixed in advance. Interpolation stayed honest throughout: 17–24 % of rows, well inside the 50 % ceiling, so the trajectories are mostly observed rather than invented.

5What is blocked

5.1Positions in metres

Both predictions about the metric chain held exactly: x_m and y_m are 100 % null across all three clips, and the pipeline logged its warning rather than guessing. That is the system behaving correctly, not failing.

The interesting question is whether the obvious workaround rescues it. Solve one homography on a reference frame, reuse it for the clip — the approach that works on a locked-off tactical camera. The test measured what that substitution actually costs, using SIFT-matched frame-to-frame homographies and a scale derived from player stature (a footballer is ~1.8 m; the detector had already measured thousands of box heights at known depths, giving ≈0.045–0.051 m/px).

A 10-metre median error is not a degraded measurement; it is the width of the penalty area. It would place a defender in the centre circle. The static homography does not rescue this angle — it spends more than 92 % of every clip outside its own error budget.

Why no heat map appears in this report

A density plot of player positions in image space is easy to produce and looks convincing. With a panning camera it is largely a picture of where the camera was pointing, not where players were. It was generated, inspected, and deliberately left out — shipping it would invite precisely the misreading this assessment exists to prevent.

5.2Physical metrics

Distance covered, top speed and sprint counts are all unavailable, following directly from §5.1 — speed is differentiated from metre positions that do not exist. Speeds are 100 % null and the per-player physical table is empty by design, exactly as predicted.

This also means the runbook's own sanity checks — 9–12 km per 90, top speed under 11 m/s — cannot be evaluated here. They are the right checks; there is simply nothing to apply them to.

5.3Player identity

Shirt numbers resolved for 0 % of rows on every clip, and player names for 0 %. At 768×432 a shirt number spans a few pixels — a human cannot read them from these frames either, at 2.4× magnification. The pipeline abstained instead of guessing, which is the correct behaviour: a guessed shirt number corrupts every per-player metric downstream, while a null merely limits you to team-level work.

No squad lists were written for these clips, deliberately. Inventing names for stock-footage players would put fabricated identities into an output table for no analytical gain.

6Corrections

One verification method used here was wrong and produced a false alarm. Recording it, because a report that only lists its successes is not evidence of anything.

The home/away mapping check C-D3 was pre-registered as a qualitative eye check and upgraded to a numerical test — comparing each assigned group's median torso chroma against the configured kit colours by Euclidean distance. On clip0 it returned SWAPPED.

It was the test that was broken. Observed "home" chroma was (123, 106) against a neutral of 128 — displaced in the blue direction, but muted, as real video colours always are. Measured by absolute distance, a muted blue sits closer to neutral grey than to saturated blue, so a white kit's neutral reference captures it. That is the exact failure the pipeline's own source comments predict: neutral references "vacuum up everything", which is why it clusters on empirical colours and compares only the centroids.

Replacing the distance test with a projection onto the away→home colour axis — invariant to muting, since muting shrinks a colour's displacement without turning it — resolved all three clips as correct, and the visual check agreed: boxes labelled home sit on blue shirts, away on white. The episode is a point in the pipeline's favour, not against it.

7What would unlock the rest

In order of leverage.

  • Use a locked-off camera. Everything in §5 dissolves the moment the camera stops moving: one homography, solved once, valid all match. This is a mounting decision, not a software problem, and it is worth more than any model upgrade below.
  • If the camera must move, a pitch keypoint model is mandatory — not an enhancement. The per-frame calibration path is already scaffolded and abstaining; it needs the model wired in. Until then no moving-camera footage yields metres.
  • Shoot at higher resolution if per-player identity matters. 768×432 forecloses shirt-number OCR entirely. Note the trade: higher resolution would also make the crowd detectable, so a spatial pitch gate becomes necessary at the same time — the free filter described in §4.1 disappears.
  • Avoid neutral kit colours for officials. clip2's grey referee was undetectable to a chroma-based matcher by construction.
  • Use a football-specific detector to get goalkeeper and referee as real classes instead of inferring them from kit colour, which would fix the official-mislabelling issue in §4.1.
  • For event data, hand-tag. The repo's own runbook already recommends it, and with metres unavailable it is the only route here.

Scope limits. The footage is rendered CGI, not real broadcast, so detector behaviour may not transfer in either direction — real players, real motion blur and real broadcast compression are all untested here. Three clips totalling 82 seconds is a feasibility probe, not a validation. The metre-scale estimate assumes 1.8 m stature and derives depth from a linear fit of box height, which is adequate for separating "0.6 m" from "10 m" but not for fine work.
